Safety in Self-Evolving AgentsA Survey of Risks, Attacks, and Governance

Self-evolving agents turn observations, feedback, and execution traces into reusable state that shapes later behavior. SAVER traces that influence as it persists, moves, and changes role across carriers — from the transition S×A that changes state, through the V→E→R chain that exposes and repairs it.

Abstract

Large language models (LLMs) exhibit strong general capabilities yet remain essentially static after deployment, failing to adapt to novel tasks, evolving environments, or dynamic contexts. In open-ended interactive environments, this characteristic motivates a shift toward self-evolving agents that continuously update reusable state: model weights, memory entries, tool definitions, or architectural components, from data, interactions, and accumulated experience.

This fundamentally changes the safety problem: when experience becomes reusable state, past events become future causes, and information that was harmless in one context may later influence decisions with greater persistence, authority, or scope. Unlike LLM and agent safety that focus on whether a response is aligned or an action is authorized, self-evolving agent safety turns to whether safety properties survive the accumulation, generalization, and cross-context reuse of locally useful experience. Existing surveys organize agent safety around static components or lifecycle stages, but rarely track how a single piece of reusable influence, once written into state, changes its carrier, role, authority, and downstream consequences as the agent continues to evolve.

To address this gap, we present SAVER, a transition-centered framework that analyzes safety through S×A→V→E→R: Substrate locates where reusable influence resides, Adaptation identifies the operation that transforms it, Violation characterizes which safety attributes are compromised, Exposure determines where and to whom the failure becomes observable, and Response evaluates whether the influence can be contained, repaired, or revoked. Treating the S×A transition as the analysis unit, SAVER traces how a given transition may induce violations, expand exposure surfaces, and demand containment responses.

Our survey reveals several key insights. Conceptually, safety failures need not originate from intrinsically harmful information; rather, otherwise legitimate state becomes unsafe when adaptation broadens its persistence, authority, or scope beyond the conditions under which it was originally valid. In terms of research coverage, existing work concentrates on early, observable stages: admission, retrieval, activation, exposure, and local containment while later stages remain sparse. Looking forward, we argue that evaluations must move beyond single-timepoint metrics toward longitudinal evidence verifying whether unsafe influence can be traced to its originating transition, contained at its exposure surface, and prevented from re-emerging after continued adaptation.

In scope

Agents that update at least one concrete state carrier after deployment; adversarial and non-adversarial risks (benign accumulation, over-generalized summaries, stale memories, failed rollback); governance mechanisms from update admission to contestable recovery.

Out of scope

A complete agent-safety survey; memory as the only core object; general continual learning of models. Prompt injection, jailbreak, backdoor, and privacy leakage appear as exposure forms rather than primitive categories.
